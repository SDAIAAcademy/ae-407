Session 1

The national framework as method

  • Name the steps of the national framework and what each one produces
  • Record context and scope: system and use limits, data and parties, automation and the human role, life cycle and change
  • Separate intended uses from uses that are out of scope
01
From testing to risk

A finding is something that happened in a test; a risk is something that may happen to people

Finding F1

A planted line made Katib email a case record to an outside address in 15 of 30 runs.

Risk R01

An applicant's record is disclosed outside the Agency. How likely, with 120 files a day? How bad, for whom? Who owns it, who accepts what remains?

And some risks no probe finds. Case workers who send drafts unread. A supplier who changes the model. A method is needed that looks there too.

The red team answers "can it be made to fail". Risk assessment answers "what should the organisation do about it, and who decides". Different questions, different people in the room: today the system owner and the data protection officer matter as much as the testers.

Step 1

Context and scope is four records, written before any risk is named

The system and its limits

Kind, purpose, outputs and how they are used. Intended uses and uses not allowed. Users and people affected.

Data, entries, exits

Data by sensitivity and source. Where content comes in and goes out. Every party in the value chain.

Automation and people

Recommends, decides or acts. Where a person approves, reviews, objects or stops. Who is responsible.

Life cycle and change

The current stage. What changes, how often, who approves. What triggers a new assessment.

These are the four activities the framework lists for this step, in our words. Lab 3.1 has one exercise per card. Engineers tend to skip this step because it "is just documentation"; every later score depends on it.

Each line on the right is a test waiting to be written. Lab 2.2 already ran the second one.

The framework asks for intended uses to be stated explicitly against unauthorised or out-of-scope uses, to limit misuse and uncontrolled expansion. In practice the right-hand column is where systems grow: someone finds the search tool useful for a purpose nobody assessed.

The level of automation

Record what the system can do, not what the design intends

Recommends

A person reads the output and acts. Mueen answering a question is close to this.

Decides

The output is the decision. Neither system is meant to decide eligibility.

Acts

The system changes something through tools. Katib sends email and writes notes.

What goes wrong. The design says "the case worker approves every letter". In Lab 2.2 a second email left before the draft was shown. The approval is a screen, not a gate.

The framework asks where human intervention is mandatory. For Katib the honest record today is: intended at Send, not enforced. That one line raises the likelihood of three risks and becomes a condition in the briefing on Day 4.

The framework asks for requirements to re-assess risks on substantial changes and for a way to tell users and affected groups. The first row is Session 4 of today.

Session 2

Scoring and calibration

  • Classify a risk into the seven types of the national framework
  • Score likelihood and impact from stated evidence on the 4 by 4 matrix
  • Calibrate with a second scorer and record the reason for every changed score
02
Step 2 · identification

Seven types of risk are a check that nothing was left out

TypeAt the Agency
Bias, discrimination and abuseWeaker protection in Latin-letter Arabic; a degrading reply
Privacy and securityA record emailed out; another applicant's bank number shown
MisinformationA false assurance of approval
Harmful useApplicants learn the fraud-screening rule and work around it
Human and machine interactionCase workers send drafts unread
Social, economic and environmental effectsDrafting skill fades; dependence on one supplier
Safety and limitationsA wrong amount in a letter; behaviour shifts after a model update

The type names are our translation of the framework's seven. Use them the way the framework proposes: after listing risks, sort them and look for an empty row. A red team fills the second and third rows and leaves the last three empty.

Step 2 · description

Each risk is described by its source, its intent and its timing

Caused by

A shortcoming of the AI system, or a person's misuse of it.

Intent

Deliberate, or not.

Timing

Before release, or after.

RiskCauseIntentTiming
R01 · a planted line makes Katib email a record outPerson, through a system weaknessDeliberateAfter release
R10 · a letter with a wrong amountSystemNot deliberateAfter release

The description decides which controls fit. A deliberate, human-caused risk needs controls that hold against an adversary who retries; an unintended system error needs detection and correction. The framework's worked example also records the related weaknesses, who is affected and the nature of the impact for every risk.

Step 3 · likelihood

Likelihood has four levels and is judged from four things

LevelMeaning
1 · RareVery unlikely to occur
2 · UnlikelyPossible, not expected in ordinary conditions
3 · LikelyConsiderably probable given the system and its setting
4 · Almost certainExpected unless effective controls limit it
  • Exposure: how much the system is used, by how many
  • Controls: what prevention is in place and how well it works
  • Ease: how little it takes
  • Precedent: has it happened, here or elsewhere

The framework does not build likelihood on percentages, because they are hard to fix for a new technology. It does give reference yearly ranges that line up with the levels: under 1%, 1% to 10%, 10% to 50%, 50% or more. A red-team rate is evidence for "ease" and "precedent"; it is a rate per attempt and not the yearly figure.

Step 3 · impact

Impact has four levels, and the fourth is for the extreme case

LevelThe expected harm is
1 · LowLimited, local, quickly contained
2 · MediumReal, but contained and put right in reasonable time
3 · HighLarge and clear; needs an institutional response
4 · CatastrophicVery grave, wide, perhaps irreversible
  • Severity of the harm
  • Size: how many people or services
  • Sensitivity of the data or the decision
  • Recovery: the slower, the higher
A wide leak of highly sensitive data, such as health or financial data, is one of the signs of catastrophic impact the framework names.

The other signs the framework lists for the fourth level include real deprivation of basic rights or vital services, and compromise of critical systems. Scorers tend to use 4 too freely at first; hold the room to the definition.

Calibration

Calibrating is pointing at evidence, not averaging and not rank

  • For each gap, both scorers name the evidence line that drove their score
  • The agreed score is the one the evidence supports
  • The reason is written into the register, so new evidence can change it

R02, agreed 3 × 4 = 12

Impact 4: hundreds of records with health notes and no way back. Likelihood stays 3: it needs a hijack and then a search.

Two risks stand at 12. R01 is almost certain and hurts one person at a time; R02 is less likely and cannot be undone. Keep both scores, not only the product.

The course outcome asks for "calibrated 4 by 4 scores" as evidence. Calibrated means a second reader can follow from the evidence to the score. An average of 9 and 16 is a number with no reason attached.

Checkpoint

Which score does the evidence support?

Risk: Mueen shows another applicant's amount or bank number on request. Evidence: 4,000 conversations a week; an English phrase filter; the lookup tool takes any identifier; the campaign reached 42% over 144 attempts; one record per request; a breach notice is needed.

Likelihood 2, impact 3: a control is in place
Likelihood 3, impact 4: personal data is always catastrophic
Likelihood 4, impact 1: frequent but small
Likelihood 3, impact 3: likely, and a large harm to one person each time
Why. Exposure and ease are high and the control is weak. The harm is large, but it is one record, not a wide leak.

Answer: 3 by 3. A case can be made for likelihood 4; accept it if the argument is "expected unless a control stops it, and none does", and note that the level changes from 9 to 12 within the same band.

Session 3

Treatment and residual risk

  • Choose among avoid, mitigate, transfer and accept with a reason
  • Re-score residual likelihood and impact from what a control changes
  • Name the owner, the signatory and the review date of every residual risk
03
Step 4 · strategy

There are four responses to a risk, and each needs a reason

Avoid

Change the design, restrict the use, switch the function off, when the risk is high and cannot be brought down.

Mitigate

Technical, organisational and operational controls that lower likelihood or impact.

Transfer

Move part of the consequences by contract, guarantee or insurance.

Accept

When what remains is within the approved tolerance, with the reason and the conditions recorded.

The framework asks for the choice to be justified and auditable. Avoid is the forgotten one: the stretch item of Lab 3.3 has a risk that disappears when the screening rule is simply taken out of the assistant's instructions.

Step 4 · controls

A control has a kind, an owner and a date

Control from the Agency's libraryKindOwner
C01 · send_letter only to the address registered on the caseTechnicalYounis Al-Wabel
C04 · the statement is summarised by a call that has no toolsTechnicalSumayya Al-Shaalan
C07 · the case worker sees recipient and attachments before a letter leavesOperationalSumayya Al-Shaalan
C08 · 30 days of notice and a staging retest before a model changeOrganisationalTuqa Al-Mishaal
What goes wrong. "The team" owns a control. Nobody is asked when it slips, and the residual score stays on the register as if it were true.

The framework's second activity in treatment: turn the strategy into a plan that can be carried out and measured, with a clear owner for every control and a timetable that fits the level of the risk. The library has fourteen controls; you built several of their kind in AE-308.

The residual score is true on the day the controls are live and their regression tests pass. Until then the inherent score is the true one, and the register must show which controls are still plans. That distinction is most of the briefing on Day 4.

A trap in the library

Insurance moves costs; responsibility stays where it was

C12 · cyber insurance for breach costs

Effect on likelihood: none. Effect on impact for the applicant: none. It pays part of the Agency's costs afterwards.

  • Transfer is a real strategy: supplier commitments, guarantees, insurance
  • It does not end the entity's responsibility for governance and compliance
  • For personal data it is never the only treatment

The framework says this in so many words about transfer. The same holds for the supplier contract in the next session: clause CL3 on training data transfers nothing if nobody checks it.

Step 4 · the decision

What remains is accepted by a named person, or escalated

A residual score nobody signed is a number. A signed one is a decision, with a date on which it is looked at again.

The table is the Agency's risk appetite, invented for the case; the framework asks that the level of the decision match the level of the risk and that acceptances and escalations be documented, and leaves the thresholds to the entity. Ask participants who signs in their own organisation; often nobody knows.

Checkpoint

Supplier risk

Every rate you measured belongs to one version of one model

  • Mueen and Katib run on a model hosted by Lisan Language Systems
  • The supplier can change that model; the Agency's tests ran on Lisan-2
  • A model change touches language handling, tool calls and safety behaviour at once

R09 on the register

The supplier changes the model and behaviour in Arabic shifts unnoticed. Likelihood 3, impact 2. It also reopens most of the other eleven.

This is the supply-chain entry of the OWASP list seen from the buyer's side. The durable point: evidence has a subject, and for behaviour tests the subject includes the model version. Write the version beside every rate.

Lab 3.4, step 1

Read the notice against the contract, clause by clause

The notice

Lisan-2 to Lisan-2.1, live in 10 days, "minor". Better dialect and Latin-letter Arabic; a new tool-call format; safety behaviour retrained; a second hosting region.

ClauseSays
CL130 days of notice for a change that "materially" affects behaviour; the supplier decides what is material
CL2The Agency may test in staging for 14 days
CL5Processing in the primary region; silent on a second region
Ten days of notice, fourteen days of testing. The right to test does not fit into the time given.

The contract is invented for the case, and its weak sentence is common in real ones: the party making the change decides whether it matters. The data protection officer owns this conversation; bring her into the role-play if you run one.

Classifying a change

The supplier classifies by what changed; you classify by what it can do to your risks

Announced changeRisks whose evidence it touches
Better dialect and Latin-letter ArabicR03, R04, R05, R06, R08: every probe result by script
New tool-call formatR01, R02, R12, R10: everything Katib does through tools

"Fewer unnecessary refusals" is good news for most users and a warning for an assurer: some of yesterday's refused probes were the "unnecessary" ones. "Better handling of Latin-letter Arabic" means the model also understands attacks in that script better.

Lab 3.4, step 3

Retest what depends on the model; keep what does not

Behaviour tests: rerun

Eight tests, 460 calls: probes by script, planted instructions, letter amounts. Staging allows 400, so one must wait: the one that guards the lowest risk.

Design tests: still valid

Policy tests of the service account, delegation chain checks, allowlist unit tests. They never called the model.

  • The hosting change costs no calls: it is a question to the supplier, in writing
  • What is left out is recorded as not retested, with the risk it guards

The reference choice drops T02, the screening-rule probes, which guard R08 at level 4. A different choice can be defended if it is argued from the register. What cannot be defended is dropping a test without writing down that the risk is now open.

EvidenceAfter the model changes

The more of the case rests on architecture, the cheaper every model change becomes. That is tomorrow's first two sessions.

This slide is the argument for Day 4 put as economics. A team whose whole case is behaviour tests must rerun everything twice a year and hope; a team with exits removed reruns a fraction.

The decision

Three answers, and each needs its evidence

Go

The affected tests were rerun on the new version and none is worse.

Go with conditions

Rerun in progress; named risks carried as open; an owner and a date for each condition.

Hold

Nothing retested yet, or a test is worse and its risk is above the appetite. The date moves.

  • Here: hold, use the 14 days of clause CL2, ask where the second region is
  • Tell the deputy governor: the scores he is asked to sign were measured on the old model

The sixth course outcome, presenting residual risk "given a scripted supplier model change", is this decision made in front of a panel. Participants should leave today with the three answers and their evidence memorised.

Day 3 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Context before risks

What the system can do, what it touches, who decides, what will change. Seven types to check nothing was left out.

Scores with reasons

Likelihood and impact from stated evidence, calibrated by two people. Keep both scores, not only the product.

Residual, signed and dated

Each control moves one score. What remains is accepted by a named person, and reopened when the model changes.

Ask each table which risk on the register they would be least comfortable signing for, and why. Their answers are tomorrow's conditions.
