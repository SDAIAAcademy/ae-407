Why review the architecture

A behaviour test asks what the model did; a design review asks what it could do

Behaviour evidence

"The planted line worked in 15 of 30 runs." A rate, of one model version, under one set of probes.

Design evidence

"The agent's account cannot send to an address that is not on the case." True for every model, every probe, every day until the design changes.

Assume the model is hijacked. Then ask what the agent's own account, placement and exits allow.

You designed these defences in AE-308. Today you sit on the other side of the table: someone else designed, and you decide whether the design supports the claim. The reviewer's stance is the attacker's: the instructions to the model are ignored, only permissions count.

The review criteria

Three questions, each with a test

Least privilege

Does the agent's account hold only what the intended uses need, on behalf of the person it acts for?

Isolation

Where does the runtime sit? Do sensitive data and an open network meet in one place?

Egress control

Which ways out exist: mail, web requests, notes others read? Is each limited to named destinations?

A fourth, monitoring, decides whether anyone would notice: actions traced to a person, and a named person who can stop the agent.

These are the criteria the lab of this session names. Each maps to risks on yesterday's register: least privilege to R02, egress to R01, isolation to both. Monitoring becomes an open item in the pack.

Agent identity

The agent has an identity of its own, and it acts for someone

The person

Case worker cw-07, signed in. Her caseload, her right to send letters, her name in the record.

The agent

Service account svc-katib. It should hold almost nothing by itself, and borrow authority for one task at a time.

What goes wrong. The agent calls every service as itself, with its own wide permissions. The case worker's limits vanish, and the log says "svc-katib" for everything.

Two questions for any agent action: for whom, and by whom. If a design cannot answer both from the request itself, least privilege is being enforced by the model's good behaviour. This is the identity half of yesterday's control C03.

Lab 4.2, steps 3 and 4

Three ways a chain breaks, and what each one gives away

Wrong audience

A token made for the case service is accepted by the letter service. Whoever captures it can replay it elsewhere.

Wider scope

The agent's token carries case:search-all, which the case worker never had. Escalation: one run reaches every case.

Subject replaced

From hop 2 the subject is svc-katib. The act is recorded as the agent's own: no accountability, no caseload limit.

These are chains A, B and C of the lab. None needs a clever attacker, and none shows up in a behaviour test: the letters still go out correctly. The second is the wildcard of Lab 4.1 seen from the token side.

Writing the check

Compare every hop with the sign-in, not with the hop before it

def check_hop(origin, hop):
    first, tok = origin["token"], hop["token"]
    broken = []
    if tok["sub"] != first["sub"]:
        broken.append("subject")
    if not tok["act"]:
        broken.append("actor")
    if tok["aud"] != hop["presented_to"]:
        broken.append("audience")
    ...
  • In chain C, hop 3 keeps the subject of hop 2
  • A check against the previous hop passes it
  • Authority comes from the person: measure against her sign-in

The lab's check has five rules and returns every rule a hop breaks. In production each service validates the token it receives; the audit question is whether it validates all five, and against what.

The limits of the evidence

The signature covers the whole system; the evidence does not

Not tested

Attachments other than text. Gulf dialect beyond a few attempts. The new model version.

Open findings

The runtime reaches the internet. Nobody is named to stop the agent.

Review date

Within 90 days, and at once on a model update, a new tool or an incident.

"Is it safe?" is answered with the page: for these uses, these risks remain at these levels, on these conditions, and this is what we did not test.

The hardest habit for a technical lead: saying what was not tested without apology and without burying it. An executive who learns of an untested area after an incident stops trusting the whole pack.
